\begin{abstract}
BitVM-style protocols verify succinct proofs on Bitcoin by garbling a proof
verifier off-chain. Existing garbled-verifier and witness-encryption designs
check elliptic-curve SNARKs, which do not resist a quantum adversary. A
hash-based STARK verifier removes that dependency but is difficult to garble:
it branches on data, its transcript is defined by an implementation, hashing
dominates its cost, and a stored circuit of $10^8$ gates does not fit in memory.
We garble the Plonky3 verifier path exercised by a Keccak-$f$ algebraic
intermediate representation (AIR) over WHIR,
using the binary tower field $\F$ and Blake3 for commitments, the transcript and
garbling. Additions are free under free-XOR, a multiplication costs $2{,}187$
\AND{} gates, a Blake3 compression costs $10{,}281$, and a streaming garbler
holds one byte per wire and one label per live wire. For a $2^{18}$-row trace at
104 bits of STARK soundness, the verifier has 94.1M non-free gates and garbles
to 1.5\,GB in under a minute on one core, about $30\times$ fewer non-free gates
than the Boolean-garbled Groth16 verifier. We then give a conditional
BitVM3-style integration and isolate its largest measured Bitcoin-side cost.
The circuit consumes
1{,}041{,}024 input bits (127.1\,KiB), distinct from the 140\,KiB serialized
proof. A policy-shaped Taproot fixture carries the raw bitstream in
33{,}299 virtual bytes (vB), but it has no signature or garbled-label
authentication and is not a protocol cost. A separate Core-tested P2TR
envelope authenticates the 130{,}128-byte payload in 33{,}004\,vB, but neither
delivers the selected labels nor includes its data-availability and fraud
graph. The accounted input-authentication component, including the adaptor or
one-time-signature witness and its verification script, measures 2.22 million
vB (MvB) for the 8-bit Schnorr-adaptor baseline or
11.85--17.27\,MvB for hash-based keys. Under strict Bitcoin Core 31.1 policy,
all three signed reveal sets were accepted after their funding transactions
confirmed. Funding plus reveals totals 2.228, 11.883 and 17.311\,MvB,
respectively; a tested Lamport join raises its bounded slice to 17.324\,MvB. The three
mechanisms still do not have identical transaction-shell boundaries, and no
complete dispute graph has been constructed; they therefore cannot substitute
for a measured total on-chain cost. The
hash-based figures also exclude the unimplemented soldering from one key set to
the seven kept garblings; the arithmetic projection for seven independent key
sets, whose consistency protocol is also unimplemented, is
82.94--120.86\,MvB before the missing graph overhead. The measured proof system provides 104 classical bits and
about 52 quantum bits, whereas the reported cut-and-choose parameters provide
about 40 classical bits and Bitcoin's transaction signatures are not
post-quantum. Thus the artifact is a measured hash-based verifier and a cost
study, not a complete post-quantum Bitcoin protocol. Recursing a zkVM proof
through a narrow tape machine, with experimental and unreviewed changes to the
proof system that we list, brings the garbled verifier's input to 564{,}552
bits in 218.0M non-free gates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bitcoin cannot verify a succinct proof economically within current Script and
policy limits, but a trust-minimized bridge needs such verification. Consider a
withdrawal from a rollup: an operator has paid out a user on Bitcoin and must
prove, to a Bitcoin script, that the rollup's state authorizes the payment; the
proof is a STARK over the rollup's execution. This bridge is the motivating
application. The implementation evaluated in this paper verifies a Keccak-$f$
AIR without public values, so it does not yet bind a proof to a withdrawal.
Hashing dominates such statements, and we evaluate on a hash-heavy standard case,
proofs of Keccak-$f$ permutations~\cite{fips202}.

A SNARK verifier written in Script takes hundreds of megabytes~\cite{babe2026}.
The BitVM line of work therefore verifies \emph{optimistically}: a prover claims
a result, and a challenger disputes it only if it is wrong.
BitVM2~\cite{laa25bitvm2} settles a dispute by bisecting the verifier's
execution trace on-chain, which is secure but expensive: a mainnet experiment
put the unhappy path at over \$14{,}000~\cite{babe2026}. BitVM3 moves the
verifier off-chain into a \emph{garbled circuit}~\cite{lin25bitvm3,bitvm3paper,che25sok}.
At setup the verifier circuit is garbled. At dispute time the proof is published
under one-time signatures that give the evaluator the circuit's input labels, and
the outcome depends on which output label the evaluation yields. For the
Groth16-sized instances reported by BitVM3 and BABE, only the signed proof and
a hashlock remain on-chain, for tens of dollars~\cite{babe2026}.

The off-chain side is where the existing designs fall short. A garbled circuit
has to be generated, stored for the lifetime of the deposit and checked, because
the garbler may cheat, typically by cut-and-choose over many instances. The
Boolean-garbled Groth16 verifier~\cite{bit25b} has about $3\times10^9$ non-free
gates, some 42\,GiB, and hours of cut-and-choose setup~\cite{babe2026}. Garbling
Groth16 with native group operations~\cite{kflt26native} and witness
encryption~\cite{babe2026} shrink the artifact to megabytes. All of these
designs, however, verify an elliptic-curve SNARK, pairing-based or its
designated-verifier form over BN254 or a binary curve: a quantum adversary breaks
them, and they need a trusted or secret setup. A STARK proof must
first be wrapped in such a SNARK, which reintroduces both.

\paragraph{Key idea.} We garble a hash-based STARK verifier directly and make it
cheap by choosing its field. Under free-XOR~\cite{ks08} a garbled circuit pays
only for non-linear gates, and in a field of characteristic two every addition
is an \XOR. A STARK over the binary tower field $\F$ with Blake3 commitments
therefore costs, as a garbled circuit, little more than its hashes and a
moderate number of field multiplications. Its algebraic checks rest on WHIR
over $\F$, while Blake3 instantiates the commitments, Fiat--Shamir transcript
and garbling PRF.

\paragraph{Challenges.} Four problems stand between this idea and a working
garbled verifier.
\begin{itemize}
  \item[C1] \emph{A fixed circuit for a branching verifier.} A garbled circuit has
    one shape for all inputs, while the verifier redraws challenges and fixes
    coordinates depending on sampled values.
  \item[C2] \emph{A transcript defined only by code.} The circuit must absorb and
    sample exactly the bytes that the prover's implementation does, or Fiat--Shamir
    soundness is lost, and that byte schedule is specified nowhere outside the
    implementation.
  \item[C3] \emph{Hashing dominates.} Merkle paths, leaf rows and the transcript
    are Blake3 compressions, and a compression costs more than four field
    multiplications.
  \item[C4] \emph{Memory.} A verifier of $10^8$ non-free gates has several
    hundred million wires; a builder that stores the gate list needs tens of
    gigabytes before garbling starts.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.} The first three contributions answer C1--C4; the
last three study its Bitcoin integration, security boundaries and measurements.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A fixed-shape circuit for the measured verifier path (C1, C2).}
    We implement the path through Plonky3's multi-STARK
    verifier~\cite{plonky3} exercised by our Keccak-$f$ AIR over the Boolean WHIR
    trace commitment as a Boolean circuit with one output wire: the transcript,
    the zerocheck of the AIR, the batching of the opened columns, the bit ring
    switch and the WHIR opening. Data-dependent branches are fixed and guarded,
    and the transcript follows a byte schedule recovered from Plonky3's own
    verifier, so one circuit serves every proof of a configuration and can be
    garbled before the proof exists (\S\ref{sec:construction}).
  \item \textbf{Cheaper gadgets (C3).} Tower-field multiplication at $3^k$ \AND{}
    gates per $2^k$ bits, with squaring and multiplication by the tower generator
    free, and a Blake3 circuit at $10{,}281$ \AND{} gates per compression instead
    of $20{,}657$, with tree mode for long inputs (\S\ref{sec:gadgets}).
  \item \textbf{A streaming garbler (C4).} A plan pass counts each wire's uses,
    and the garbling pass garbles and checks each gate as the builder emits it and
    releases a wire's slot after its last use (\S\ref{sec:streaming}).
  \item \textbf{A Bitcoin integration study and explicit deployment gaps.} We
    price three ways to publish the proof, sweep the proof parameters, and
    separate the accounted one-key-set input-authentication component from the
    unimplemented multi-copy soldering and transaction graph
    (\S\ref{sec:protocol}). Including signature or preimage witness data and
    authentication scripts, this component measures 2.22\,MvB with the 8-bit
    adaptor baseline and 11.85--17.27\,MvB per hash-key set, against a few kilobytes
    for Groth16. These are not total on-chain footprints. Tuning removes only a
    fifth of the logical input.
  \item \textbf{A scoped quantum analysis.} We analyze the STARK and garbling
    components in the quantum random-oracle model, including Grover search on
    the garbling offset, and separate those bounds from the lower
    cut-and-choose level and the non-post-quantum Bitcoin transaction layer
    (\S\ref{sec:pq}).
  \item \textbf{Measurements.} Real Keccak-$f$ proofs from $2^5$ to $2^{20}$ rows,
    a gate profile, an ablation of the gadget and memory choices, measurements
    of the one-key-set input-authentication components, seven-key-set
    projections, a comparison with pairing-based designs, and the garbled
    verifier of a recursion proof of Ziren's binary stage at 564{,}552 input
    bits (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}
The circuit builder and the measurements are open source; the streaming
garbler, the Blake3 circuit and a label-level evaluator are contributed to
GOAT's bitvm-gc~\cite{bitvmgc}.

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Garbled verifiers on Bitcoin}

We follow the BitVM3 setting~\cite{bitvm3paper}. A \emph{garbler}, the operator
who proves, and an \emph{evaluator}, a challenger, agree on a verifier circuit $C$
with one output wire, and $C$ is garbled at setup. At dispute time the operator
publishes its proof on-chain under one-time keys, and each published bit gives
the evaluator the input label of that bit's value. The proof is public, so we use
\emph{privacy-free} garbling: the evaluator may learn every wire's value. What
garbling must provide is \emph{authenticity}. Holding the labels of an input $x$,
the evaluator obtains the label of the output value $C(x)$, and it cannot obtain
the label of the other value. A hashlock on the label of value 0 lets a challenger
who evaluates an invalid proof stop the operator's claim.

The garbler may cheat, so the garbling is checked by cut-and-choose, as in
BABE~\cite{babe2026}, Mosaic~\cite{mosaic26} and bitvm-gc~\cite{bitvmgc}; its cost
is linear in the size of the garbled circuit, which this paper reduces.
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} gives an integration sketch, accounted
input-publication components and the unresolved deployment requirements.

\subsection{The garbling scheme}\label{sec:garbling}

We use privacy-free garbling~\cite{fno15privacyfree} with free-XOR~\cite{ks08}
and one ciphertext per non-free gate, in the syntax of Bellare, Hoang and
Rogaway~\cite{bhr12foundations}.

Let $\Delta$ be a random
secret 128-bit offset. Every wire $w$ has a false label $w_0$, and its true label
is $w_1=w_0\oplus\Delta$. Let $H(\ell,g)=\blake(\ell\,\|\,g)$ truncated to 128
bits, where $g$ is the gate's index. An \XOR{} gate sets $c_0=a_0\oplus b_0$ and
has no ciphertext. An \AND{} gate $c=a\wedge b$ sets
\[
  c_0 = H(a_0,g), \qquad \mathit{ct} = H(a_1,g)\oplus H(a_0,g)\oplus b_0 .
\]
An evaluator holding the label $a$ of value $x$, and $b$ of value $y$, outputs
$H(a,g)$ if $x=0$ and $H(a,g)\oplus\mathit{ct}\oplus b$ if $x=1$. In both cases
this is the label of $x\wedge y$. An \textsf{OR} gate is symmetric, with
$c_0=H(a_1,g)\oplus\Delta$ and $\mathit{ct}=H(a_1,g)\oplus H(a_0,g)\oplus b_1$.
The garbled circuit is one 16-byte ciphertext per \AND{} or \textsf{OR} gate.

Two details matter in practice. First, $\Delta$ must be secret and fresh for
each garbling: with a public $\Delta$ any label yields its complement, and the
output's other label is free to compute. Second, a hash-based PRF gives labels
no algebraic structure. RSA-based garbling proposals for BitVM3 were broken
exactly through such structure: label forgery by extended Euclid on coprime
exponents, and by the Franklin--Reiter related-message
attack~\cite{flb25}.

\subsection{The proof system}

We target Plonky3's multi-STARK verification pipeline~\cite{plonky3} over the
Boolean WHIR trace commitment. The evaluated implementation instantiates one
AIR, with no public values, preprocessed columns or lookups, and requires
current- and next-row openings for every column. The pipeline uses fields in the
Wiedemann tower~\cite{wiedemann88}
\[
  \T_0=\mathbb{F}_2,\qquad \T_{k+1}=\T_k[X_k]/(X_k^2+X_{k-1}X_k+1),\qquad X_{-1}=1 ,
\]
up to $\T_7=\F$. An element of $\T_{k+1}$ is stored as its low half (the
coefficient of $1$) and its high half (the coefficient of $X_k$), recursively
down to bits. The trace is committed as bits: a Boolean column is packed into
$\F$ elements, and WHIR commits to the packed multilinear polynomial on an
additive (Cantor) evaluation domain~\cite{cantor89,lch14}. Binary towers and
ring switching come from Binius~\cite{dp25binius,dp24ringswitch}.

\paragraph{Terms.} An \emph{AIR} (algebraic intermediate representation) is a
set of polynomial constraints over a trace's current and next rows. A
\emph{zerocheck}~\cite{hyperplonk23} proves that the constraints vanish on every
row by a sumcheck~\cite{lfkn92}
against the equality polynomial $\mathrm{eq}(\tau,\cdot)$ at a random point
$\tau$. \emph{WHIR}~\cite{whir25} is a Reed--Solomon proximity test used as a multilinear
polynomial commitment. Its \emph{STIR queries}~\cite{stir24} open the committed, folded
codeword at random positions, and its \emph{out-of-domain} (OOD) samples bind the
committed polynomial at points outside the evaluation domain. The \emph{Cantor
basis} is the $\mathbb{F}_2$-basis $v_0=1$, $v_i^2+v_i=v_{i-1}$, which gives
binary fields an additive FFT domain. The \emph{bit ring switch} turns a claim
about a polynomial's bits into a claim about its packed $\F$ representation.
\emph{Johnson list-decoding regime} refers to the proximity parameters for which
the soundness bounds are proven via the Johnson bound, without conjectures.

\paragraph{Verification.} The verifier:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item runs a \emph{zerocheck} of the AIR: a degree-4 sumcheck over the row
    variables, then the AIR constraints, evaluated at the bound point from the
    opened current and next rows and Horner-batched under a challenge $\alpha$,
    against $\mathrm{eq}(\tau,r)$;
  \item batches the opened columns into one bit-level claim, at a column point
    drawn from the transcript;
  \item runs the \emph{bit ring switch}, which turns that claim into an $\F$
    claim on the packed polynomial. It uses a $128\times128$ tensor and, above
    $2^7$ rows, its carry and last tensors, a batched sumcheck, and a closing
    weight computed in the tensor algebra;
  \item runs the WHIR opening of that claim: sumcheck rounds with proof of work,
    STIR queries on Merkle-committed folded codewords, and a final polynomial.
\end{enumerate}
All challenges come from a Blake3~\cite{blake3spec} sponge over bytes, and all
Merkle trees use Blake3 compressions.

\subsection{Gadgets}\label{sec:gadgets}

\paragraph{Tower arithmetic.} A wire vector for an element of $\T_k$ is its
bit pattern, so gadget outputs can be read back directly as field elements.
Multiplication follows the tower recursively, Karatsuba at each level:
\[
  z_0=a_0b_0,\quad z_2=a_1b_1,\quad z_1=(a_0+a_1)(b_0+b_1)+z_0+z_2,\qquad
  ab=(z_0+z_2)+(z_1+X_{k-1}z_2)\,X_k .
\]
Additions are free. Multiplication by the generator $X_{k-1}$ is a rewiring plus
\XOR{}s, and squaring is linear over $\mathbb{F}_2$. The only \AND{} gates are
the base-field products, three per level, so a $2^k$-bit multiplication costs
$3^k$ \AND{} gates: $2{,}187$ in $\F$. A multiplication by a constant folds to
\XOR{}s entirely.

\paragraph{Blake3.} We start from bitvm-gc's Blake3 circuit and change two
things. A 32-bit addition's carry is the majority
\[
  \mathrm{maj}(a,b,c)=c\oplus\big((a\oplus c)\wedge(b\oplus c)\big),
\]
one \AND{} per bit, and the carry out of the top bit is discarded, so it is not
computed. This halves a compression from $20{,}657$ to $10{,}281$ \AND{} gates.
We also add Blake3's tree mode (chunk chaining values on a stack, parent nodes,
the root last). The verifier's transcript absorbs more than one 1{,}024-byte
chunk between samples: the final polynomial is 64 coefficients, 1{,}024 bytes
on its own. Table~\ref{tab:gadgets} gives the costs. Every gadget is checked
against the reference implementation (\texttt{p3-binary-field}, the
\texttt{blake3} crate) on random inputs.

\subsection{Transcript}

The transcript is Plonky3's challenger over a Blake3 byte sponge. Observing
appends to an input buffer; sampling, when the output buffer is empty, hashes
the input buffer, replaces it with the digest, and pops bytes from the digest's
end. A field element is 16 little-endian bytes. A query index is 8 bytes read
as a little-endian integer and masked. A proof-of-work check absorbs the
witness and requires a fixed number of sampled bits to be zero. We implement the
same challenger on wires. Its schedule has no data-dependent branch: queries are
a fixed number of masked draws, and a proof-of-work check a fixed number of
bits. So the circuit's shape is a function of the configuration alone. The
sub-transcripts' domain separators are constants, baked in as constant wires,
and everything the prover sends is an input.

The byte schedule, that is, the order in which roots, out-of-domain answers,
sumcheck messages, proof-of-work witnesses, stratified query draws and the final
polynomial are absorbed and sampled, is implicit in Plonky3's implementation and
not specified separately.
We recovered it by running Plonky3's own verifier through a challenger that logs
every byte, and we test the circuit against that log op for op.

\subsection{The multi-STARK layers}

The zerocheck, the column batching and the ring switch are implemented
gate for gate from a reference implementation that is checked op for op against
Plonky3's verifier. The AIR arrives as Plonky3's symbolic constraints and is
evaluated on wires.

Two of Plonky3's data-dependent branches cannot appear in a
fixed circuit. We fix them and guard them with checks.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Plonky3 redraws a zerocheck challenge coordinate that happens to be zero.
    The circuit takes a fixed number of draws and requires each to be nonzero.
  \item Plonky3's ring switch would fix a column-point coordinate that is
    exactly $0$ or $1$. The circuit assumes there is no such coordinate and
    checks it.
\end{itemize}
Each event has probability about $2^{-127}$ per coordinate. When it occurs the
circuit rejects where Plonky3 would continue, never the reverse.

\subsection{WHIR}

The WHIR layer checks every proof-of-work witness. It hashes every opened row and
walks it up its Merkle path to the root absorbed in the transcript. It then folds
each queried row with the round's folding randomness, and checks the final
queries against the final polynomial and the closing sumcheck identity. Plonky3 sends
Merkle openings pruned: siblings that another query can recompute are left out.
Our implementation expands the proof outside the circuit into one full path per
query. This is an engineering choice rather than an inherent limitation of a
fixed circuit: a fixed upper-bound circuit could verify a padded frontier with
data-dependent routing. A million-trial simulation of the actual stratified
queries and cap forest, however, reduces the expected path from 1{,}354 to
1{,}335.71 digests, only 4{,}683 input bits or $0.45\%$ of the total, before the
routing and padding overhead. When the commitment uses a Merkle cap of $2^h$
roots, the transcript
absorbs all of them, and each query selects its root with a multiplexer on the
top $h$ index bits: $256(2^h-1)$ \AND{} gates, against $h$ compressions saved.

\section{Streaming garbling}\label{sec:streaming}

The circuits here have $10^8$--$10^9$ non-free gates and several times as many
wires. A builder that stores the gate list, as bitvm-gc's \texttt{CircuitAdapter}
does, needs tens of gigabytes before garbling starts. Upstream's own DV-SNARK
verifier test, for example, exceeds 64\,GB. Nothing in garbling needs the gate
list, though: a gate's ciphertext depends only on its input labels.

Our garbler is a circuit-builder backend that garbles each gate as it is
emitted. It uses two passes of the same deterministic builder
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:stream}). The \emph{plan} pass records, for each wire by
creation index, the number of gates that read it: one byte per wire, with 255
meaning ``never release''. The \emph{garbling} pass hands out slots. A wire's
slot is released after its last read, and a later wire takes it. Wire
identifiers are slots, which the builder interface permits. The two constant
wires, and any wire read after the build (an output), are pinned. Both passes
apply the same constant folding ($x\oplus x=0$, $x\wedge 1=x$, \ldots), so they
see the same wires. Slot storage is chunked, so that a growing array never
reallocates.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Streaming garbling of a deterministic builder $\mathcal{B}$}
\label{alg:stream}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{plan:} run $\mathcal{B}$ on a backend that only counts: $\mathit{uses}[k]\gets$ reads of the $k$-th created wire
\State pin the constants and the outputs: $\mathit{uses}[k]\gets\infty$
\State \textbf{garble:} $\Delta\gets\{0,1\}^{128}$; run $\mathcal{B}$ again on the backend:
\State \quad on input wire $k$: $\ell_0[s]\gets\blake_{\mathit{seed}}(k)$ in a free slot $s$; $v[s]\gets$ its value
\State \quad on $\XOR(a,b)$: $\ell_0[s]\gets\ell_0[a]\oplus\ell_0[b]$; $v[s]\gets v[a]\oplus v[b]$
\State \quad on $\AND(a,b)$ with gate index $g$:
\State \qquad $(c_0,\mathit{ct})\gets\mathrm{Garble}(\ell_0[a],\ell_0[b],g,\Delta)$; emit $\mathit{ct}$
\State \qquad $e\gets\mathrm{Eval}(\ell_{v[a]}[a],\ell_{v[b]}[b],\mathit{ct},g,v[a])$
\State \qquad $\ell_0[s]\gets c_0$; $v[s]\gets v[a]\wedge v[b]$; \textbf{assert} $e=\ell_{v[s]}[s]$
\State \quad on $\textsf{OR}(a,b)$: as for \AND{}, with the \textsf{OR} garbling of \S\ref{sec:garbling}
\State \quad after each read of $a$: decrement $\mathit{uses}[a]$; release $a$'s slot at zero
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

When the garbler holds a witness, it evaluates as it garbles. At each non-free
gate it runs the evaluator's formula on the labels of the actual values, and
requires the result to equal the label of the gate's output value. At setup no
proof exists, and none is needed: garbling a gate uses only its input labels,
so the garbler runs the garbling pass with every input held at 0 and keeps the
ciphertexts. A challenger later evaluates them with an evaluator backend that runs
the same builder on the proof and holds one label per wire. We tested exactly
this. Garbled with every input at 0 and then evaluated, the stored garbling gave
the label of 1 on a real proof and the label of 0 on the same proof with one
opened value changed, at $2^5$ and $2^8$ rows. Evaluation took 11.5\,s and
17.0\,s, against 20.6\,s and 34.0\,s for garbling, since it hashes once per
non-free gate where garbling hashes twice. Memory is one byte per wire for the plan, 0.61\,GiB for the 659M wires at
$2^{18}$ rows, plus 18 bytes per live wire (a 16-byte label, the value and a use
counter), 41\,MB at the peak of 2.26M live wires. The plan depends only on the circuit, so every garbled
instance of a configuration can share it.

\section{Security}\label{sec:security}

This section states what the garbled verifier guarantees and on which
assumptions. It combines the soundness of the proof system, the effect of the two
guarded branches, and the authenticity of the garbling.

\paragraph{The circuit and the verifier.} When neither guarded event of
\S\ref{sec:construction} occurs, the circuit computes exactly Plonky3's verifier;
we test this op for op against Plonky3's logged transcript and on real proofs.
When a guarded event occurs, the circuit outputs 0. Hence an output of 1 implies
that Plonky3's verifier accepts, and an honest proof is rejected only if a guarded
event occurs, with probability at most $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{guard}}\approx
n\cdot2^{-127}$ for $n$ guarded coordinates.

\paragraph{Soundness of the proof system.} We use Plonky3's own security
assessment of each configuration as the input to our classical accounting. The
proximity terms are in the Johnson list-decoding regime
(Table~\ref{tab:security}). The quantum conversion in \S\ref{sec:pq}
additionally assumes round-by-round soundness for the sumcheck-based layers.
At $2^{18}$ rows the binding term is the WHIR opening at $104.3$ bits (110 bits per
term, rate $1/32$, folding factor 4, proof of work up to 32 bits). The other terms
are constraint batching $109.0$, zerocheck $114.5$ and its sumcheck $113.5$, bit
ring switch $114.0$, column batching $115.2$, and commitment and transcript
collisions $128$ (Blake3). We write $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{STARK}}(t)$ for the
composed error against an adversary making $t$ hash queries. Plonky3's report
states it as bits of security, $104.3$ at $2^{18}$ rows, meaning that
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{STARK}}(t)\le t\cdot2^{-104.3}$ for a classical adversary; \S\ref{sec:pq} gives the quantum bound.

\paragraph{Authenticity of the garbling.} Our scheme is privacy-free
garbling~\cite{fno15privacyfree} with free-XOR~\cite{ks08} and one ciphertext per
non-free gate~\cite{zre15}, with $H=\blake$ and a fresh secret $\Delta$ per
garbling. Free-XOR is secure when $H$ is circular correlation
robust~\cite{ckkz12freexor}; we assume this of Blake3, as is standard for
hash-based garbling. In the BitVM setting the garbled circuit is published at
setup and the proof is chosen afterwards, so authenticity must hold for inputs
chosen adaptively, after the garbled circuit is seen~\cite{bhr12adaptive}. Let
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GC}}$ be the advantage of an evaluator that sees the garbled
circuit, then chooses an input $x$, receives its labels, and outputs the label of
the value $\neg C(x)$ on the output wire. We assume that our Blake3-based
free-XOR garbling has adaptive authenticity in this sense, with Blake3 modelled as
a random oracle; Bellare, Hoang and Rogaway give constructions proven adaptively
secure~\cite{bhr12adaptive}.

\noindent The following circuit-level statement is conditional on the Blake3
circular-correlation-robustness and adaptive-authenticity assumptions stated
above. It does not cover cut-and-choose, input soldering or Bitcoin transaction
enforcement.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $C$ be a correctly garbled verifier of a fixed configuration, and $x$ the
encoding of a proof $\pi$ for a statement.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] \emph{Soundness.} If the statement is false, an evaluator holding the
    labels of $x$ obtains the output label of value 0, except with probability at
    most $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{STARK}}(t)$, where $t$ bounds the hash queries of
    the party that chose $\pi$.
  \item[(ii)] \emph{Authenticity.} If $\pi$ is honestly generated, an evaluator
    holding the labels of $x$ obtains the output label of value 0 with probability
    at most $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{guard}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GC}}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} (i) If $C(x)=1$, Plonky3's verifier accepts $\pi$ by
the previous paragraphs, which for a false statement happens with probability at
most $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{STARK}}(t)$. Otherwise $C(x)=0$, and correctness of the
garbling yields the label of value 0. (ii) For an honest proof $C(x)=1$ unless a
guarded event occurs, and the label of value 0 is then the label of $\neg C(x)$,
which the evaluator obtains with probability at most
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GC}}$. \hfill$\square$

\smallskip\noindent The theorem applies only to the relation represented by
$C$. In our experiments that relation is Keccak-$f$ trace validity without
external public values; it does not prove authorization of a bridge withdrawal.

\smallskip\noindent Section~\ref{sec:protocol} uses (i) to protect challengers
against a false claim and (ii) to protect an honest operator.

\subsection{Post-quantum analysis}\label{sec:pq}

The verifier construction avoids number-theoretic assumptions: the proof
system, commitments, transcript and garbling use Blake3. This statement does
not cover Bitcoin transaction authorization or every proposed input-publication
mechanism. The estimates below are conditional on the stated random-oracle,
round-by-round-soundness and adaptive-authenticity assumptions; they are not an
end-to-end post-quantum security proof.
Pairing-based verifiers (Groth16, Pari and their designated-verifier variants),
the witness-encryption schemes built on them (\S\ref{sec:related}) and Bitcoin's
own Schnorr signatures fall to Shor's algorithm~\cite{shor94}. Our verifier does
not. Avoiding discrete-logarithm and pairing assumptions is necessary for
quantum resistance here, but a hash-based verifier is not sufficient by
itself: a quantum adversary still speeds up generic preimage and collision
search~\cite{grover96,bht98}. Table~\ref{tab:pq} gives component-level estimates.

\paragraph{Fiat--Shamir against quantum adversaries.} In the quantum random-oracle
model, Chiesa, Manohar and Spooner~\cite{cms19qrom} show that applying
Fiat--Shamir with a hash to a public-coin interactive oracle proof with
round-by-round soundness error $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rbr}}$ bounds the success
of a quantum adversary that makes $t$ hash queries by
$O(t^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rbr}}+t^3/2^{\lambda})$, where $\lambda=256$ is the
digest length. A classical adversary is bounded by
$O(t\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rbr}})$.

WHIR has round-by-round soundness~\cite[\S5]{whir25}. For the
sumcheck-based layers (zerocheck, column batching, ring switch) we assume it, as
is standard for sumcheck, with each of Plonky3's terms as the round error. A
round with error $2^{-a}$ therefore offers about $a/2$ bits
against a quantum adversary, and Grover search~\cite{grover96} attains this
generically when the bad challenges form an unstructured set. Proof-of-work
grinding is generic search too: $b$ bits of grinding cost $2^{b}$ hashes
classically and about $2^{b/2}$ quantumly.

\paragraph{The proof system at our parameters.} At $2^{18}$ rows each WHIR term
targets 110 bits, of which grinding supplies 26 to 32 bits (per round: 33
queries with 30 and 28 bits of grinding, 20 with 32 and 30, 15 with 29 and 32, and
12 terminal queries with 27). Both parts halve under quantum search, so each WHIR
term offers about 55 bits, and the composed $104.3$ bits become about 52. The
algebraic terms (zerocheck $114.5$, its sumcheck $113.5$, ring switch $114.0$,
column batching $115.2$, constraint batching $109.0$) are Schwartz--Zippel errors
over the $2^{128}$-element field and offer 54 to 58 bits. They are capped by the
field: no number of queries raises them. Collisions in the 256-bit Blake3
digests cost about $2^{85}$ quantum queries~\cite{bht98}, and preimages
$2^{128}$.

\paragraph{The ceiling of the current configuration space.} Raising Plonky3's
per-term target does not raise the quantum level. We searched the target that
the proven-list-decoding profile accepts at $2^{18}$ rows. Up to 134 bits per term
it keeps the queries at $33+20+15+12$ and raises the grinding to 51--56 bits,
its ceiling; at 200 bits it refuses, needing 103 bits of grinding. Plonky3's
security report fully assesses targets up to 117 bits per term, where the
composed soundness is $108.6$ bits, bound by constraint batching at $109.0$, a
field-size term no WHIR parameter moves. Grinding and the field-bound terms both
halve under quantum search, so no configuration of the current proof system
exceeds about 54 quantum bits. Reaching a quantum target is a change to the
proof system, not to its parameters.

\paragraph{The garbling.} Authenticity rests on the secrecy of $\Delta$, and
$\Delta$ can be tested against a single gate. An evaluator holding the labels
$a,b$ of an \AND{} gate with known values $x,y$ and its ciphertext $\mathit{ct}$
checks a candidate $\Delta'$ by recomputing $\mathit{ct}$ from
$a,a\oplus\Delta',b$ and $\Delta'$. A wrong candidate passes with probability
$2^{-128}$. Grover search therefore recovers $\Delta$ with about $2^{64}$
quantum evaluations of Blake3, after which every label's complement is known
and the output label of the other value is free. With 128-bit labels the
garbling offers about 64 bits against a quantum adversary. The same holds for a
hashlock on a 128-bit output label: a preimage search over $2^{128}$ labels costs
about $2^{64}$. Grover search is highly sequential, and the adversary has only the
time between setup and the end of the dispute window, but we do not rely on
this.

\paragraph{The surrounding protocol.} The protocol of \S\ref{sec:protocol}
publishes input bits under one-time keys. Lamport and Antichain Winternitz keys
rest on \texttt{HASH160} preimages. The familiar $2^{64}$ quantum cost for our
16-byte preimages is a single-target estimate. One Lamport key set exposes
$T=1{,}041{,}024\approx2^{20}$ alternative preimages, so generic multi-target
search costs about $2^{128}/T\approx2^{108}$ classically and
$\sqrt{2^{128}/T}\approx2^{54}$ quantumly. Seven independent sets lower these
figures to about $2^{105.2}$ and $2^{52.6}$. Hash-chain alternatives have the
same multi-target issue; Table~\ref{tab:pqsel} gives the widths that lift the
selection itself above this bound. Even 20-byte preimages under \texttt{HASH160} are
capped near 70 quantum bits for one set, rather than 80, once the target count
is included.
The surrounding protocol is not post-quantum as instantiated. Schnorr adaptor
signatures, the committee's pre-signed transactions and Taproot key-path spends
fall to Shor's algorithm. Hash-based input keys avoid the adaptor-signature
dependency, but their one-key-set cost assumes the unimplemented soldering in
\S\ref{sec:protocol}; the seven-key-set projection is 37--54 times the
adaptor payload. Bitcoin has no opcode that natively verifies a post-quantum
signature. QSB gives a non-standard hash-based authorization path under
existing consensus rules~\cite{levy26qsb,starkware26qsb}, but whether it can
replace the authorization of a pre-signed, timelocked dispute graph is outside
our scope. We therefore treat graph authorization as an explicit open
assumption rather than a solved component.

\paragraph{Parameters for a quantum target.} There is no single rule that
doubles every classical parameter. Fiat--Shamir and preimage-search terms need
about $2\lambda_Q$ classical bits, generic collision search on an $n$-bit digest
costs about $2^{n/3}$ quantum queries, and an information-theoretic
cut-and-choose error must itself be at most $2^{-\lambda_Q}$. Seven changes
follow.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The WHIR terms need about $2\lambda_Q$ bits each. Since grinding also
    halves, the query count must carry most of it: from about 80
    information-theoretic bits per term today to about 168 at $\lambda_Q=100$,
    about $2.1\times$ the queries. Plonky3's profile raises grinding instead,
    so this needs a query-heavy profile.
  \item The algebraic terms need challenges from a field of about
    $2^{2\lambda_Q+14}$ elements, which means one more tower level,
    $\mathbb{F}_{2^{256}}$, at $3^8=6{,}561$ \AND{} gates per multiplication, three
    times today's. Repeating each algebraic check with independent challenges is
    an alternative at a similar cost.
  \item Labels of $2\lambda_Q$ bits: 256-bit labels double the ciphertext size to
    32 bytes per non-free gate. The gate count is unchanged, because labels feed
    the garbling hash, not the circuit.
  \item Merkle commitments and the transcript need a hash that withstands
    $2^{\lambda_Q}$ quantum collision search. Generic collision search therefore
    calls for at least a $3\lambda_Q$-bit digest and a sufficiently wide internal
    state. Blake3-256 caps this component near 85 quantum bits; requesting a
    longer XOF output does not widen its 256-bit chaining value. A replacement
    hash circuit and its cost remain to be designed and measured.
  \item For $T$ possible input-authentication targets, 100 quantum bits require
    at least $200+\lceil\log_2 T\rceil$ bits of preimage entropy under a hash
    wider than 160 bits: about 220 bits for one key set and 223 for seven.
    Byte-aligned 28-byte preimages under \texttt{SHA256} meet this generic
    target, but that construction and its larger witness cost remain to be
    designed and measured.
  \item Cut-and-choose needs parameters satisfying
    $1/\binom{N}{M}\le2^{-\lambda_Q}$, together with an ungrindable uniform
    selection of the opened instances. The setup time and kept-instance storage
    must be recomputed for those parameters.
  \item The pre-signed graph needs post-quantum transaction authorization.
    Bitcoin currently has no general post-quantum signature opcode. QSB gives a
    non-standard hash-based spend under existing consensus rules, but adapting
    it to the committee-authorized, timelocked graph is unstudied here.
\end{itemize}
Applying only the first three factors to the measured $2^{18}$ verifier profile
gives a partial lower-bound estimate, not a 100-bit configuration measurement.
Merkle paths and leaf hashes scale with the query count ($2.1\times$), and leaves
also with the element size ($2\times$), to about 45M gates. The transcripts
absorb twice the bytes, about 24M. Multiplications triple, and those that depend
on the queries also scale with them, to about 213M. The result is about 280M
non-free gates, $3\times$ today's, and about 9\,GB garbled at 32 bytes per gate.
It leaves Blake3-256 and its 85-bit quantum collision ceiling unchanged and
excludes the replacement hash circuit, stronger cut-and-choose, input
authentication and graph authorization. It is therefore not an estimate of a
100-bit end-to-end protocol.

\paragraph{Setup.} The operator garbles $N$ instances of $C$ from independent
seeds. Before any instance is opened, it commits for every instance $j$ the
hash of its garbled tables, $h_j=\mathrm{SHA256}(\ell^j_0)$, the hash of the
label of output value 0 (reject), and the instance's input-key material: the
hashes of its one-time keys and its label translations. A challenger then
chooses $N-M$ instances to open, re-garbles each opened instance from its seed
and checks every commitment of that instance; only then is the transaction
graph signed. The streaming garbler re-garbles in one garbling pass per instance, reusing the plan that all
instances share (\S\ref{sec:streaming}). The challenger stores the $M$ kept
instances, 1.5\,GB each at $2^{18}$ rows. The intended protocol fixes one-time
keys for the input bits of $C$. Publishing bit $b$ of input $i$ must give a challenger only the label of
value $b$ on wire $i$ in every kept instance. Mosaic provides this correlation
for its adaptor-signature path~\cite{mosaic26}; the corresponding hash-based
soldering is open here. The intended transactions would be pre-signed at setup
by a committee, as in BitVM2~\cite{laa25bitvm2}, whose enforcement needs one
honest signer.

\paragraph{Required transaction semantics.} The intended branches are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Kickoff}: the operator claims reimbursement for a withdrawal it has
    paid. If no challenge follows within $\Delta_C$, \emph{Take1} pays it. This is
    the happy path.
  \item \emph{Challenge}: any challenger may spend Kickoff's challenge output,
    paying the operator a bond that covers the cost of Assert, as BitVM2's
    crowdfunded challenge does~\cite{laa25bitvm2}.
  \item \emph{Assert$_1,\dots,$Assert$_k$}: the operator must publish every
    authenticated circuit-input bit. Because the payload exceeds one standard
    transaction, a concrete construction needs a subgraph whose terminal output
    is spendable only after every chunk has been published. We do not specify
    that fanout, chaining or completion mechanism here.
  \item \emph{AssertTimeout}: if the operator fails to create the completion
    output before $\Delta_A$, a timeout branch should transfer its stake. The
    enforceable condition for ``all chunks published'' depends on the missing
    subgraph and is not specified by Figure~\ref{fig:graph}.
  \item \emph{Disprove$_j$}: after such a completion output exists, a challenger
    holding $\ell^j_0$ would open the corresponding hashlock before $\Delta_D$;
    otherwise Take2 would pay the operator. The required transaction
    dependencies and relative timelocks remain unimplemented.
\end{itemize}
All three authenticated rows below now serialize complete signed reveal
transactions and execute their selected leaves with real BIP341 sighash
contexts. Each fixture uses BIP341's unknown-discrete-log NUMS internal key, so
there is no known key-path secret that bypasses the measured script leaf. Their
output shapes still differ, but all three sets have been replayed through
Bitcoin Core's strict standard-policy mempool after funding confirmation; only
the Lamport fixture includes a signed join. Additional graph inputs and outputs,
non-authentication scripts and control blocks, graph-authorization signatures,
anchors, timeouts, dependencies and fee-management overhead are not uniformly
included. Current Bitcoin Core policy limits a standard transaction to
400{,}000 weight units and an unconfirmed cluster to 101 virtual
kilobytes~\cite{bitcoincorepolicy}; the complete graph must be tested against
the release policy targeted for deployment.

The evaluated Keccak-$f$ circuit has no public values and therefore does not
instantiate the bridge statement. A bridge deployment must bind the withdrawal,
rollup-state commitment and other public claim data into both the AIR relation
and Fiat--Shamir transcript. We have not implemented or measured that
extension; its circuit and input costs are outside the reported figures.

\paragraph{Intended dispute.} In a completed instantiation, a challenger would
read the published bits, obtain the corresponding input labels for every kept
instance, and evaluate each instance. Evaluation takes one Blake3 call per
non-free gate and runs off-chain: 17\,s at $2^8$ rows
(\S\ref{sec:streaming}). If the proof is invalid, a correctly garbled instance
yields $\ell^j_0$, which is intended to open Disprove$_j$. The circuit size
affects off-chain evaluation, while the proof size controls the Assert payload.
This flow is conditional on the missing soldering and transaction subgraph.

\paragraph{Security.} Let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cc}}$ bound the probability
that every kept instance is malformed while every opened one is correct; for
choosing $N-M$ of $N$ uniformly this is $1/\binom{N}{M}$, which is $2^{-40}$ at
$(N,M)=(181,7)$~\cite{babe2026}. Let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sol}}$ bound a failure of
the soldering, and let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sig}}$ bound the aggregate
probability of forging any one-time or transaction-authorization signature
across the entire dispute. It is not a per-key probability; a reduction from a
per-target bound must include the number of available targets. At these
parameters, cut-and-choose alone limits
the intended malicious-garbler security to about 40 bits, irrespective of the
STARK verifier's 104-bit classical soundness. Subject to an enforced transaction
graph, timely confirmation and an instantiated soldering bound,
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} supports the following intended properties.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A false claim is disproved.} If the operator's statement is false, an
    honest challenger obtains some $\ell^j_0$ except with probability
    $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{STARK}}(t)+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cc}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sol}}$:
    a correctly garbled kept instance exists, its labels are those of the published
    proof, and Theorem~\ref{thm:main}(i) applies. An operator that publishes no
    proof loses its stake to AssertTimeout.
  \item \emph{An honest operator keeps its stake.} If the operator's proof is
    honest, no challenger obtains any $\ell^j_0$ except with probability
    $M(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{guard}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GC}})+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sol}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sig}}$,
    by Theorem~\ref{thm:main}(ii) and a union bound over the kept instances.
\end{itemize}
This is conditional accounting, not a protocol theorem in a ledger model:
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sol}}$ has no instantiated bound, and the transaction
graph and ledger-timing assumptions have not been implemented or proved.
BitVM3~\cite{bitvm3paper} and BABE~\cite{babe2026} analyze their own
instantiations.

\paragraph{Label selection.} Every input mechanism below is an instance of one
two-party task, which we state once so that the three baselines and the
hash-based alternative can be compared on the same terms. The
\emph{garbler} $\mathsf{G}$ (the operator, who also proves) holds, for every
input wire $w$ of the kept garblings, the two labels $\ell_w^0,\ell_w^1$. The
\emph{evaluator} $\mathsf{E}$ (a challenger) will run the garbled verifier on
the proof $\pi$. Publishing $\pi$ on-chain must therefore (i)~\emph{deliver} to
$\mathsf{E}$ the label $\ell_w^{\pi_w}$ of every input wire, (ii)~be
\emph{exclusive}: no party learns $\ell_w^{1-\pi_w}$ for any $w$, since a
single complementary label breaks authenticity (\S\ref{sec:security}),
(iii)~be \emph{binding}: $\mathsf{G}$ can publish at most one value per wire,
and the publication is at the same time the authorization of the spending
transaction, and (iv)~be \emph{public}: any observer can recover $\pi$ and
check that the published object is well formed. A malformed publication does
not satisfy the Assert script and cannot be spent, and a missing one forfeits
the operator's stake through AssertTimeout; Disprove is reserved for the reject
label. Lamport achieves (i)--(iv) with one
preimage per bit, and its cost is the per-bit script. Figure~\ref{fig:adaptorseq}
is the adaptor construction of BitVM3 and Mosaic~\cite{bitvm3paper,mosaic26},
which achieves them with one signature per $k$-bit digit and no per-bit script.
Figure~\ref{fig:hashseq} replaces the signature by a pair of hash-chain
openings and is the construction behind the Antichain row and the hash-based
projections of \S\ref{sec:pq}.

The two protocols publish the same amount of selection material per digit:
one 64-byte completion for Figure~\ref{fig:adaptorseq}, two $n$-byte nodes for
Figure~\ref{fig:hashseq}. At $k=8$ and $n=16$ both are 8 bytes of
witness per bit, and indeed the 130{,}128 adaptor completions and 16{,}266
64-bit hash records of our fixtures are both exactly 8{,}458{,}320 bytes. They
differ in where the remaining cost sits. The adaptor's selection is verified by
one \texttt{CHECKSIG} per digit, 3 bytes of script, and its binding is the same
signature; its setup holds $2^k$ secrets and ciphertext bundles per digit, and
its exclusivity rests on discrete logarithms, which Shor's algorithm removes
(\S\ref{sec:pq}). The hash-based selection has a two-node setup per digit and
hash-only exclusivity, but under current consensus every hash step of the
verification is 2 bytes of script, plus a separate transaction signature. That
script is what makes the Antichain row 5.3 times the adaptor row in
Table~\ref{tab:onchain}; the digit-to-label translation table is the same
object in both protocols and is not on-chain.

\paragraph{A post-quantum instantiation.} Figure~\ref{fig:hashseq} becomes
a post-quantum replacement for the adaptor under three changes, none of which
is available on Bitcoin today. First, the forward hashing moves out of Script
into a native batch verifier: one leaf per transaction carries the batch root
$\rho$, the first digit index and the count, and an opcode re-derives every
endpoint from the records on the stack. Second, the transaction-binding
signature becomes hash-based; the draft P2MR output type and its 0xC2 leaf
version\footnote{BIP360, \url{https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0360.mediawiki}, and the SHRINCS proposal for a stateful hash-based signature in that leaf; both are drafts and may change.}
would provide a 548-byte stateful hash-based signature per transaction and
6{,}000-byte stack items. Third, the node width $n$ must match the quantum
target: forging a different digit from a published opening is a second-preimage
search over the roughly $2^{22}$ chain nodes that the whole input makes
public, so $n=16$ gives about 53 quantum bits, $n=24$ about 85 and $n=32$ about
117. We serialized this construction exactly with rust-bitcoin for $n=16$ and
$n=24$ at $k=4$, including the P2MR outputs, 0xC2 leaves, placeholder
signatures, depth-one control blocks and canonical multi-record stack items,
and tested the pair code and the batch parser against tampering, reordering,
cross-batch splicing and padding; nothing in it is executable by Bitcoin Core.
Table~\ref{tab:pqsel} gives the results together with a projection whose model
reproduces both serialized fixtures within $0.03\%$. The operative row is
$n=32$, $k=8$: full-width nodes, about 117 quantum bits for the selection, and
the same 2.13\,MvB as the Schnorr adaptor, at the price of up to 510 hash
steps per byte inside the opcode, about $6.6\times10^7$ compressions for the
input. Its setup is two seeds per digit rather than the adaptor's $2^k$
secrets, so widening $k$ does not grow the choice table; only the translation
table grows, to $2^k\cdot k\cdot 16$ bytes per digit, 4.3\,GB per instance at
$k=8$. The delivery and soldering of that table across the $M$ kept
garblings is the same open item as for every other row.

\paragraph{Publishing the input.} We evaluate three baseline one-time schemes for carrying the input
bits, and they trade on-chain size against assumptions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Lamport.} Each bit has two hashed 16-byte preimages, and the
    revealed preimage is the one-time-signature witness. The leaf script
    per bit,
    \begin{center}\ttfamily\small OP\_HASH160 OP\_DUP $h_0$ OP\_EQUAL OP\_SWAP $h_1$ OP\_EQUAL OP\_BOOLOR OP\_VERIFY\end{center}
    is 49 bytes, and the witness 17 bytes. We executed it with 998 bits per
    input, and it rejected wrong preimages. The transaction-bound fixture
    prefixes each leaf with a 32-byte authorization key and
    \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIGVERIFY}, and supplies a 64-byte
    \texttt{SIGHASH\_DEFAULT} signature. Its initial stack has 999 items and
    the measured transient peak is exactly the 1{,}000-item Tapscript
    limit~\cite{bip342}. Complete one-output reveal
    transactions give $16.59$\,vB per input bit. The reported row uses a
    one-leaf tree with a 33-byte control block; timeout siblings and their
    longer control paths belong to the missing complete graph.
  \item \emph{Antichain Winternitz.} Plain Winternitz chains are unsafe here: from
    a published chain value the evaluator hashes forward to the labels of other
    digit values~\cite{antichain26}. Antichain Winternitz closes this and costs
    a cited marginal $11.31$\,vB per bit at parameters $(2,16)$, counting the
    hash-chain opening witness and authentication script but cancelling fixed
    transaction overhead. Our transaction-bound fixture pins the upstream
    implementation, prepends a 64-byte Schnorr authorization, and includes two
    P2TR outputs, input shells, leaf scripts and depth-zero controls. With at
    most 332 four-bit digits per input, 784 inputs in 131 transactions cost
    11{,}848{,}875\,vB in the all-nonzero worst case, or $11.38$\,vB per input
    bit. Zero-valued ScriptNum coordinates only reduce this figure. The source
    reports $0.256$\,kB of off-chain table per bit. Its
    larger parameters save more on-chain but need up to $43.8$\,kB per bit
    off-chain~\cite{antichain26}, 46\,GB for our input.
  \item \emph{Schnorr adaptor signatures} over 8-bit digits, as in BitVM3 and
    Mosaic. BitVM3 reports 9{,}707 weight units (WU) for a 1{,}024-bit Assert
    transaction with one input and two P2TR outputs~\cite{bitvm3paper}, or
    $2.37$\,vB per bit at that size. Its printed witness formula instead sums to
    9{,}451 WU once the SegWit marker and flag are included, while the merged
    64-byte-\texttt{SIGHASH\_DEFAULT} implementation implies 9{,}323 WU for
    that shell. Our executable fixture reproduces 9{,}323 WU, uses the BIP342
    opcode index rather than a byte offset for every code separator, and reaches
    the exact 998-signature stack capacity. Core 31.1 validates all completed
    signatures. Integer packing of 130{,}128 eight-bit digits uses 133
    inputs in 23 two-output transactions and totals 2{,}222{,}213\,vB, or
    $2.13$\,vB per input bit. Using the minimum 131 inputs instead needs 26
    transactions and is 184\,vB larger. Mosaic's source construction solders all
    kept instances to one set of signatures by polynomial label correlation,
    with an on-chain footprint independent of the number of
    instances~\cite{mosaic26}. This repository tests only the one-key-set
    transaction fixture; it does not implement or test Mosaic's polynomial
    correlation or setup verification. The signatures rest on
    discrete logarithms, and an evaluator that holds the signing key can spend
    without completing the adaptor~\cite{antichain26}.
\end{itemize}
Packing more bits into each adaptor completion reduces the serialized
transaction shape. Our Core-tested 16-bit fixture uses 2{,}960\,vB of funding
and 1{,}111{,}128\,vB across 12 reveals, or 1{,}114{,}088\,vB in 13
transactions. Exact serializations of 10-, 11- and 12-bit digits, still
with placeholder signatures and not replayed in Core, give 1{,}782{,}498,
1{,}620{,}499 and 1{,}485{,}410\,vB including funding. The price is off-chain:
a table of every digit's $2^{b}$ choices at 65 bytes each is 2.165\,GB per key
set at 8 bits, 6.929, 12.598 and 23.097\,GB at 10, 11 and 12 bits, and
277.162\,GB at 16. This is an encoding sensitivity result, not yet a
binary-label authentication mechanism: the complete $2^{16}$ choice table and
exclusive digit-to-binary-label delivery remain unimplemented. We therefore retain the
8-bit construction as the authenticated baseline in Table~\ref{tab:onchain}.
Our labels are free-XOR correlated, so a hash-based preimage $s$ cannot itself be
a label. A key-derivation function (KDF) maps it to one by a translation ciphertext
$\mathrm{KDF}(s)\oplus w_b$, two per input bit and instance: 32 bytes per bit,
or 33\,MB per instance at $2^{18}$ rows. Using one on-chain key set for all $M$
kept instances requires a proof that the translations of both opened and kept
instances encode the same bit choices. BABE's zero-knowledge
soldering~\cite{babe2026} and Glock's verifiable secret
sharing~\cite{eag25glock} provide related mechanisms at smaller input sizes,
but we have neither constructed nor costed one for a million input bits.
Consequently the one-key-set hash rows in Table~\ref{tab:onchain} are
conditional on an unimplemented mechanism, and
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sol}}$ is presently unbounded.

A conservative component projection authenticates each input independently for
every kept instance, multiplying the accounted authentication component by $M$. This is
not itself an implemented or proved protocol because the same bit choice must
still be enforced across all key sets. At $M=7$, simple multiplication projects
the accounted Lamport component to 120.86\,MvB and the all-nonzero Antichain
component to 82.94\,MvB,
before transaction-graph overhead.

\paragraph{Executed components.} We ran the dispute components on a small circuit, with its
on-chain steps executed as Bitcoin Script by \texttt{bitcoin-scriptexec}. The
statement is knowledge of a 64-byte $x$ with $\blake(x)=h$: one Blake3
compression, 512 input bits and 10{,}536 non-free gates. The operator garbles it
at setup, before choosing $x$, and publishes Lamport hashes, translation
ciphertexts, the labels of the two constant wires and $\mathrm{SHA256}$ of the
reject label. Its Assert publication runs through a 25\,KB Lamport script under the
1{,}000-item stack limit. The challenger derives the input labels from the
published preimages and evaluates the stored garbling without $\Delta$ or any
label it was not handed. For a true $x$ it obtains the accept label, which the
Disprove hashlock script rejects; for $x$ with one bit changed it obtains the
reject label, which opens it. Assert rejects a preimage outside the committed
keys. We then ran the same dispute on the Keccak-$f$ STARK verifier at $2^5$
rows. Garbled with every input at 0, it reads 586{,}240 input bits, which the
operator publishes through 588 Assert scripts of at most 998 bits each,
28.7\,MB of script in all, each executed under the stack limit. The challenger's
evaluation of the stored garbling from the published labels gives the accept label
on the real proof and the reject label, which opens Disprove, on the proof with
one opened value changed. Signatures, timelocks and cut-and-choose are not part
of either run. These runs validate individual input-publication,
label-translation, evaluation and hashlock components for one garbling. They do
not validate Figure~\ref{fig:graph}, multi-instance soldering, transaction
policy or ledger-level security.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

The experiments answer five questions: how the garbled verifier scales with the
trace (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}), where its gates go
(Table~\ref{tab:profile}), how much each design choice contributes (the
ablation), how the circuit-input authentication component projects and how far it shrinks
(Tables~\ref{tab:onchain} and~\ref{tab:inputs}), and how it compares with
pairing-based designs (Table~\ref{tab:compare}).

\paragraph{Setup.} We proved Keccak-$f$ permutations with Plonky3's
1{,}625-column characteristic-2 Keccak AIR (booleanity is not constrained,
since the commitment is to bits). A byte-logging challenger recorded each run,
our reference replayed Plonky3's verifier byte for byte, and the measured
verifier circuit was then streamed through the garbler. The parameters were a
rate of $1/32$, a folding factor of 4, and a target of 110 bits per WHIR term.
Garbling was single-threaded. Every run up to $2^{18}$ rows was allocated
32\,GB of RAM and eight cores on an Intel Xeon Platinum
8375C at 2.9\,GHz; the $2^{20}$ run, whose proof took longer to generate, ran on
an AMD EPYC 9355 limited to 128\,GB and 32 cores.

\paragraph{Artifact availability.} The source repository includes the locked
dependencies, benchmark commands, raw measurement logs and their SHA-256
manifest. The commands and memory caps are listed in
\texttt{whir-gc/README.md}, and \texttt{paper/data/README.md} maps tables and
figures to raw logs, and \texttt{patches/} holds the experimental Plonky3 and
Ziren changes behind Table~\ref{tab:recursion}. \texttt{Cargo.lock} pins Plonky3 at revision
\texttt{ca4da2daa2e0dfdd31419af5c870813083398955} and bitvm-gc at revision
\texttt{10083d4a63eb08a344dc40ee84bc11394b218a89}. Gate, wire and input counts
are deterministic; wall-clock times are single-run engineering measurements
on the machines above unless a range is stated.

The circuit's size depends on the AIR: the transcript, the column combination
and the constraint evaluation grow with the number of columns and constraints.
All sizes below are therefore for this 1{,}625-column AIR, which is a demanding
case. For a fixed verifying key and public-input arity, a Groth16 verifier's
circuit does not grow with the proved trace length.

\paragraph{Scaling.} Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} give the
measured verifier configuration from $2^5$ to $2^{20}$ rows. Every circuit accepted its proof, and
at $2^5$ and $2^8$ we also checked that it rejected the proof with one opened
value changed. Growing the trace $32{,}768$-fold grew the circuit $2.2$-fold,
because the verifier depends on the trace length only through the logarithmic
number of variables.

\paragraph{Garbling efficiency.} To compare garbled verifiers across trace sizes
and AIRs, we define the \emph{garbling efficiency} of a configuration as
\[
  \eta \;=\; \frac{|\mathsf{GC}|}{R\cdot W}\quad\text{bytes per committed cell},
\]
where $|\mathsf{GC}|$ is the garbled circuit's size in bytes, 16 per non-free
gate, $R$ the number of trace rows and $W$ the number of committed columns. It is a
cost per unit of verified computation, so smaller is better. For our Keccak-$f$
AIR, $W=1{,}625$ and each cell is one bit. Because $|\mathsf{GC}|$ grows with
$\log R$ while $R\cdot W$ grows with $R$, $\eta$ falls almost in inverse
proportion to the trace: from $13{,}715$ bytes per cell at $2^5$ rows to $3.53$ at
$2^{18}$ and $0.94$ at $2^{20}$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}). At $2^{20}$ rows this is
less than one garbled byte per committed bit: 7.5 garbled bits per bit, or
38\,kB per Keccak-$f$ permutation. A verifier whose size is fixed with respect
to trace length, such as Groth16 for a fixed verifying key and public-input
arity, has $\eta$ inversely proportional to the computation its proof covers as
well; comparing $\eta$ across proof systems therefore needs the same
computation and cells of the same kind, bits here against field elements in a
prime-field AIR.

\paragraph{WHIR alone, and its parameters.} Garbling only the WHIR opening of a
$18$-variable polynomial (rate $1/32$, folding 4, $35+22+16$ queries) cost
$24.3$M non-free gates with single-root commitments, and $21.2$M with a Merkle
cap of 32 roots. That cap is Plonky3's recommended height and was the optimum in
our sweep of 1 to 256 roots. Three quarters of the WHIR circuit was hashing.
Across rates $1/16$--$1/64$ and folding factors 2--5, rate $1/32$ with folding 4
gave the smallest circuit among the configurations we kept; rate $1/64$ saved a
further $10\%$ of gates at the price of a prover domain twice as large.

\paragraph{Why a binary field.} We also built KoalaBear
($p=2^{31}-2^{24}+1$) and Poseidon2 on wires, to price the prime-field
alternative. A KoalaBear multiplication is $3{,}451$ \AND{} gates, and a
degree-4 extension multiplication $60{,}615$, against $2{,}187$ for all of
$\F$. One Poseidon2 permutation of width 16 is $1{,}240{,}747$ \AND{} gates:
$120$ Blake3 compressions. The WHIR opening of a $2^{20}$ KoalaBear
configuration (2{,}157 permutations, about 7{,}500 extension multiplications)
would be about $3.1\times10^9$ gates with Poseidon2, more than the whole
Boolean-garbled Groth16 verifier, and still $0.3$--$0.5\times10^9$ with Blake3,
against our $21$M for a $2^{18}$-variable opening. The four-fold difference in
size does not account for a gap of $15$ to $150\times$: a garbled STARK verifier
should use a binary field.

(127.1\,KiB), whereas the serialized proof is 140\,KiB. Every authenticated row
includes its signature or opening witness, transaction-binding authorization,
authentication script and complete reveal-transaction shell. The adaptor and
Antichain fixtures use two P2TR outputs per transaction; Lamport uses one. The
table therefore compares concrete
input-publication components, but not complete or identical dispute graphs.

The executable raw-data fixture provides a transport reference point, not a
lower bound. It puts
the same logical bits into 1{,}627 80-byte witness items over two one-leaf P2TR
inputs. The serialized transaction includes witness framing, both drop scripts,
both 33-byte control blocks, two input shells and one P2TR output; it is

For one key set, the executable fixtures measure 2.22\,MvB with the 8-bit
Schnorr-adaptor baseline, 11.85\,MvB with safe Antichain Winternitz and 17.27\,MvB with

serializes every field of 174 one-output transactions: 1{,}044 P2TR inputs,

scripts and depth-zero control blocks. All selected leaves execute with real

one-set hash rows require unimplemented soldering;

82.94--120.86\,MvB projections, without proving consistent bits.

\paragraph{Narrow statements and batching.} Holding the height fixed while
narrowing the AIR discards most of the represented computation, so we instead
required at least as many committed cells as the measured
$1{,}625\mathbin{\times}2^{18}$ trace and rounded the height up to a power of two.
Under the current WHIR profile, hypothetical widths 128 and 32 at heights
$2^{22}$ and $2^{24}$ require 659{,}840 and 636{,}288 input bits, reductions of
$36.6\%$ and $38.9\%$. Combining those layouts with the best configuration in
the rate/folding/48-bit-grinding sweep gives 464{,}768 and 441{,}216 bits,
reductions of $55.4\%$ and $57.6\%$. These are exact schedule counts for layout
models, not generated proofs: no such narrow Keccak AIR is implemented, the
power-of-two rounding represents $126.03\%$ of the original cell count, and the
aggressive result inherits the quantum-search and prover-cost caveats of
grinding.

Batching has stronger evidence because both endpoints are real artifacts. The
$2^{18}$ and $2^{20}$ traces contain 10{,}485 and 41{,}943 Keccak-$f$
permutations, while their verifier inputs are 1{,}041{,}024 and 1{,}108{,}992
bits. Thus $4.0003\times$ as much work increases the absolute input by only
$6.53\%$, reducing the input per permutation from 99.29 to 26.44 bits, a
$3.76\times$ amortization. Batching does not reduce the absolute unhappy-path
payload for one proof, but it is the only measured input optimization here that
requires no new AIR or input-delivery protocol.

cheaply. Ziren's binary stage~\cite{ziren} proves, over $\F$ with Boolean WHIR
and Blake3, the program that verifies a shrunk zkVM proof, and its
\texttt{binary-recursion} crate records Plonky3's verifier of such a proof as a
straight-line tape over $\F$. A five-table tape machine (ledger, arithmetic,
rewiring, Blake3 message and round tables) proves a run of a tape, and its own
verifier is recorded and proved again: narrow recursion. We translate a tape

binary stage's verifier has 3.37G non-free gates and 18{,}050{,}304 input bits;

The steps combine recording choices with changes to the proof system. The
recorder reads opened values the verifier knows (padding and constant
preprocessed columns) as constants, and reads the fixed preprocessed Merkle
tree down to 18 layers below its root as circuit constants, chosen by a one-hot
of the query's top index bits. The schedule folds 4, 5, 4, 4 variables per WHIR
round at 46 bits of grinding. The protocol changes are five. Ring-switch claims
at points sharing the coordinates one packed element absorbs send one tensor,
weighted by powers of a challenge drawn after the points, and a pair's main and
preprocessed switches run as one over both packings behind a selector variable.
Merkle digests are Blake3 truncated to 200 bits, which keeps collisions at
$2^{100}$ work classically and $2^{66.7}$ under BHT search. Each table sends one
value per linear form of its columns that its constraints and buses read,
derived from the symbolic AIR, and a sumcheck over the column index reduces them
to the table's ring-switch claim; the verifier rebuilds the columns with a
constant right inverse, which costs only \XOR{}s. Preprocessed forms are cut
to those independent over the fixed trace's GF(2) column basis and the
all-ones column, the rest rebuilt by an affine inverse, and one sumcheck over
the widest table's column index serves every table. Bus product-GKR rounds send
the round polynomial without its linear eq factor. Each change passes Plonky3's
and Ziren's tests and tamper tests of its own, but none has been reviewed; the
patches are in the repository. Each proof meets 100 bits of composed soundness
by Plonky3's assessment in the Johnson regime, with up to 46 bits of grinding
per term at level 4, which halves under quantum search. The circuit checks only
the last proof, so the statement's soundness also composes over the earlier
levels. Proving level 4 alone took 1.8 hours on 64 cores with four GPUs
grinding and about 470\,GB of memory. Serializing the input-publication fixtures of
Table~\ref{tab:onchain} at 564{,}552 bits, with every transaction signed and
every leaf executed under the stack limit, gives 1{,}205{,}217\,vB for the 8-bit
adaptor baseline (73 inputs in 13 reveal transactions), 6{,}425{,}757\,vB for
Antichain $(2,16)$ (426 inputs in 71) and 9{,}363{,}254\,vB for Lamport (566
inputs in 95), plus a 7{,}155\,vB Lamport join; Disprove is one 97\,vB
transaction. Plain 4-bit Winternitz, modeled from one executed input at
23.06 weight units per bit, would be about 3.26\,MvB, but it cannot deliver
garbled labels without a translation layer. These reveals were not replayed in
Bitcoin Core, and the accounting boundary is that of
Table~\ref{tab:onchain}.

valid proof and rejected it with one witness bit flipped; built as a stored
circuit, the same verifier needed more than 64\,GB. The recursion verifier
we compare (Table~\ref{tab:recursion}) has $2.2\times$ fewer non-free gates,
and about $14\times$ fewer than the Boolean-garbled Groth16 verifier; the

On-chain, the comparison inverts. The Groth16 designs publish 508 to 1{,}019
input bits and report dispute paths of a few to a few tens of kilobytes, under

orders of magnitude more (about $38\times$ deferred binding's 32\,kvB with
adaptors), before the unconstructed remainder of the
graph. That is the price of verifying the STARK directly rather than wrapping
it, and \S\ref{sec:protocol} and \S\ref{sec:discussion} are about reducing it.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

We group prior work by how it verifies a proof on Bitcoin: by garbling a verifier,
by witness encryption, or through small pairing-based SNARKs that both approaches
rely on. We close with the garbling techniques we use.

\paragraph{BitVM and garbled verifiers.} BitVM2~\cite{laa25bitvm2} verified
optimistically by bisecting the verifier's trace on-chain. BitVM3 garbled the
verifier instead~\cite{lin25bitvm3,che25sok}, and its formal treatment defines
BitVM3-CORE as a sound and complete on-chain proof system with on-chain costs of
about \$9~\cite{bitvm3paper}. The Boolean-garbled Groth16 verifier~\cite{bit25b}
has about $3\times10^9$ non-free gates, 42\,GiB, with cut-and-choose setup of over
three hours at $(N,M)=(181,7)$~\cite{babe2026}. Garbling Groth16 with native
elliptic-curve group operations reduced the garbled program to
2.4\,MiB~\cite{kflt26native}. Glock~\cite{eag25glock} formalized garbled locks and
garbled a designated-verifier variant of Pari; BABE reports its garbled circuit at
about 12\,MB on a binary elliptic curve~\cite{babe2026}. GOAT's
bitvm-gc~\cite{bitvmgc} garbles Groth16 and a designated-verifier form of
Pari~\cite{garudapari} over BN254 and over the binary curve sect233k1. We garble a
measured STARK-verifier path with the same garbling code, and we contribute our
streaming garbler and Blake3 circuit back to it. BitVM3's comparison of garbling schemes already lists a
Yao-style garbled STARK verifier, at about 10\,GB of off-chain storage and as an
estimate~\cite[Table~9]{bitvm3paper}; we build one and measure 1.5\,GB per
instance. TRAPGC-DV~\cite{trapgcdv26} verifies Groth16 by designated evaluation:
the prover wraps its proof in an outer Groth16 proof under a per-session trapdoor
SRS and garbles a small circuit that checks it in scalar form, with
cut-and-choose and a universal-composability analysis. It too rests on pairings.

\paragraph{Garbling on Bitcoin: security and inputs.} Futoransky, Larotonda and
Barbara~\cite{flb25} broke the authenticity of the RSA-based garbling proposed for
BitVM3~\cite{lin25bitvm3}, of the label forward-propagation variant~\cite{fdz25},
and of linear adaptors: coprime public exponents let the evaluator forge labels by
extended Euclid, and linear adaptors fall to the Franklin--Reiter related-message
attack. Our hash-based garbling has no such label algebra. Mosaic~\cite{mosaic26}
made cut-and-choose maliciously secure with an on-chain footprint independent of
the number of garbled copies, and Antichain Winternitz~\cite{antichain26} published
input labels on-chain at a cost up to $52.9\%$ lower than Lamport signatures. Both
are independent of the garbled circuit; \S\ref{sec:protocol} prices both for our
million-bit input.

\paragraph{Quantum-safe transaction authorization.}
QSB~\cite{levy26qsb} constructs a hash-based authorization path under existing
legacy-Script consensus rules by combining HORS-style commitments with
transaction-bound hash-to-signature grinding. A QSB spend was mined on Bitcoin
mainnet in August 2026~\cite{starkware26qsb}. The construction is non-standard
and requires direct miner inclusion. It authorizes a specially constructed
spend; adapting it to a multi-transaction, pre-signed and timelocked dispute
graph remains open.

\paragraph{Verifying a STARK in Script.} Our companion work, in the same
repository as this paper's code, verifies the same kind of proof directly in
Bitcoin Script, with Poseidon2 over KoalaBear and WHIR and without
\texttt{OP\_CAT}~\cite{goatscriptverifier}. It needs no garbling and no
trusted party, but its $2^{20}$ example configuration requires 1.24\,GB of
script, equivalent to 309 blocks; moreover, a standard transaction holds less
than one Poseidon2 permutation, so it must be split into sub-query steps.
Garbling moves that cost off-chain.

\paragraph{Witness encryption on Bitcoin.} BABE~\cite{babe2026} replaced the
garbled Groth16 verifier with witness encryption for linear pairing
relations~\cite{gkpw24}. It made the one quadratic pairing $e(\pi_1,\pi_2)$ linear
by computing $r\pi_1$ in a small garbled circuit, built from decomposable
randomized encodings on top of Argo MAC~\cite{el26argo}. The per-instance artifact
is 22\,MiB, the on-chain cost is comparable to BitVM3, and cut-and-choose setup
takes seconds. Deferred binding~\cite{goatpbwe} extended BABE to public inputs
known only at dispute time: a second garbled circuit computes a blinded point
$Q=x_D L_D+B$ from the late input and a third computes $rQ$, relying on
Groth16's linear public-input aggregation. Its implementation reports 0.92\,GiB
per finalized instance, 768 input labels, and an Assert of 2{,}154 bytes with a
14{,}312-byte ChallengeAssert witness.

\paragraph{Witness encryption for covenants.} Bitcoin PIPEs v2~\cite{pipesv2}
witness-encrypt a Schnorr signing key under an NP statement to emulate covenants
without a soft fork. They define witness signatures, and they map BitVM's disprove
condition to a relation $f(w)=0\wedge h=\mathrm{Hash}(w)$, which requires the
witness at setup. Their witness encryption comes from arithmetic affine
determinant programs~\cite{aadp2026}, an arithmetic variant of affine determinant
programs~\cite{bij20adp}. The ciphertext grows as $v(2g+1)^2$ field elements,
cubic in the constraint count, which is about 338\,TB for a purpose-built
14{,}371-constraint pairing-SNARK verifier. Security is heuristic: an earlier
version fell to a rank-drop attack, and the linearization analysis is described
as inconclusive. All witness-encryption designs above are smaller off-chain than a
garbled verifier, but all require the statement to be a pairing SNARK, so a STARK
would first have to be wrapped in one, and none is post-quantum.

\paragraph{Small SNARK verifiers.} Pari~\cite{garudapari} has the smallest known
pairing-based proofs, two $\mathbb{G}_1$ and two field elements, with a
three-pairing verifier. Its equifficient polynomial commitments also give Garuda,
a prover with free linear gates. Pari underlies bitvm-gc's designated-verifier
SNARK, Glock, and the SNARK of the AADP construction; Glock and bitvm-gc's
\texttt{sect233k1} verifier use its designated-verifier form on a binary curve,
which needs no pairing but still rests on discrete logarithms. Groth16~\cite{gro16} remains
the deployed baseline.

\paragraph{Binary-field proof systems.} Binius~\cite{dp25binius} builds succinct
arguments over towers of binary fields, and ring switching~\cite{dp24ringswitch}
turns a claim about a polynomial's bits into one about its packed extension-field
form; Plonky3's Boolean WHIR commitment, which we garble, uses both ideas with
WHIR~\cite{whir25} as the proximity test. Its cheap additions are what make a
garbled verifier small.

\paragraph{Garbling.} We use privacy-free garbling~\cite{fno15privacyfree} with
free-XOR~\cite{ks08}, one ciphertext per \AND{} gate~\cite{zre15}, and the syntax
of Bellare, Hoang and Rogaway~\cite{bhr12foundations}; free-XOR's security rests on
circular correlation robustness~\cite{ckkz12freexor}. Witness encryption in general
goes back to Garg, Gentry, Sahai and Waters~\cite{ggsw13}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why the operator garbles.} The roles could be swapped, with a
challenger garbling and the operator evaluating its own proof to obtain an accept
label that unlocks its payment. The operator then needs the input labels of its
proof from the garbler. Publishing both labels of every input wire would disclose
$\Delta$, their XOR, and with it every label; handing them over on request lets
the garbler withhold them, so that the operator's payment depends on the
challenger's cooperation. With the operator as garbler, a withheld label only
hurts the party that withholds it.

\paragraph{Input authentication dominates the measured components.} The measured verifier and one-copy Script
components use primitives available today, but we have not shown that the
complete multi-transaction protocol is enforceable under current relay policy.
The three signed funding--reveal slices are enforceable only in confirmed
stages under Core 31.1; none is an atomic unconfirmed graph. Only the Lamport
fixture also constructs and tests a join.
The circuit input has 1{,}041{,}024 bits (127.1\,KiB), distinct from the 140\,KiB
serialized proof. The accounted dispute-time authentication component consists
of the signature or opening witness, its verification script and the
row-specific transaction envelope; it is 2.22--17.27\,MvB for one key set,
with 2.22\,MvB denoting the 8-bit adaptor baseline
(Table~\ref{tab:onchain}). The reveal fixtures have explicit but not identical
output shapes and are not total on-chain costs. The hash-based
one-set figures require unimplemented multi-copy soldering, and the independent
$M=7$ component projection is 82.94--120.86\,MvB before the missing graph. Among
the components measured here, the raw bits fit either the unauthenticated
33.3-kvB fixture or a 33.0-kvB signed envelope, but neither supplies selected
GC labels. Label-authenticated reveal rather than byte transport or the Taproot
commitment itself is therefore the bottleneck.
WHIR tuning removes only a fifth of the input (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}); equal-area
narrow-statement models remove $37$--$58\%$, but require a new AIR, and the
larger real trace shows that batching can amortize input per permutation by
$3.76\times$ without reducing the absolute logical circuit input. Narrow
recursion reaches $0.54\times$ the input with real proofs
(Table~\ref{tab:recursion}), at the price of a recursion prover and of
proof-system changes still to be reviewed.
Deployability additionally requires public-input support and a concrete,
serialized and policy-tested transaction graph.

\paragraph{Conditional input compression.} TinyLabels compresses honest
selected-label delivery to $|x|+\operatorname{poly}(\lambda)$ online bits under
Ring-LWE in the random-oracle model~\cite{tinylabels24}. Its reported concrete
batch supports 699{,}050 128-bit labels with an approximately 56-KB selector
key, so the present input would need two reported-size batches: roughly
0.24\,MB for the selector keys and bit vector before Bitcoin encoding. This is
a useful communication frontier, not a drop-in replacement for Table~\ref{tab:onchain}.
The selector key is not publicly verifiable, and the malicious-2PC wrapper
turns malformed delivery into an abort rather than public evidence. A malicious
or withheld key can therefore leave the evaluator with garbage labels but no
reject label that opens \textsf{Disprove}; Bitcoin Script cannot cheaply check
the underlying Ring-LWE relation. A publicly verifiable or penalizable
Batch-Select, including consistency across the $M$ kept garblings, remains an
open input-layer direction.

\paragraph{Segmented verification.} Another architecture would commit once to
a fixed serialization of the proof data, locate a faulty verifier segment by
bisection and authenticate only the challenged chunk. For $N=1{,}041{,}024$
bits split into chunks of at most $B$ bits under a 256-bit Merkle tree, its
data-only challenged input is
\[
 B+256\left\lceil\log_2\left\lceil N/B\right\rceil\right\rceil+C_{\rm state},
\]
where $C_{\rm state}$ binds the transcript, accumulator and boundary state.
For example, a 6{,}656-bit first-round query chunk has 157 chunks and an
eight-digest path, hence 8{,}704 bits before state. Query segmentation alone is
insufficient: leaf rows and paths account for 510{,}464 input bits, leaving a
530{,}560-bit global remainder containing the opened rows, sumchecks, ring
switch and transcript. Achieving the bound requires segmenting those stages,
binding their boundary states and constructing a fault-location and withholding
penalty protocol. None is implemented here; without those mechanisms the
worst-case disclosure remains the full $N$ bits.

\paragraph{Other AIRs.} Our sizes are for the 1{,}625-column Keccak AIR. Plonky3
also has binary AIRs for SHA-256 and Blake3, but they read no next-row columns,
which our verifier currently assumes; supporting them changes the opening layout
and is left to future work. The column-dependent terms, about a third of the
circuit, scale with the AIR's width.

\paragraph{Verifying the garbling.} A garbler must be kept honest, and in
current deployments that is cut-and-choose over many instances. Its cost is
linear in the garbled circuit. At $(N,M)=(181,7)$ and $2^{18}$ rows, the
operator garbles 181 instances at about 57\,s each, 2.9 core-hours or about
11 minutes on 16 cores, and a challenger stores the 7 kept instances, 10.5\,GB.
BitVM3 reports 3 hours 24 minutes on 16 cores and 288.4\,GB for the
Boolean-garbled Groth16 verifier at the same parameters~\cite{bitvm3paper}.
Against native-group garbling of Groth16~\cite{kflt26native} or BABE, whose
artifacts are megabytes, our setup is larger. The streaming garbler re-garbles
an opened instance from its seed in one garbling pass, reusing the plan that all
instances share. These setup figures are derived from the measured garbling time,
not measured end to end.

\paragraph{Further reductions.} The ring switch closing is a third of the
circuit and is the first target. An $\F$ multiplier below Karatsuba's
$3^7$ is a second: bitvm-gc's $\mathbb{F}_{2^{233}}$ multiplier, which evaluates,
multiplies and interpolates over $\mathbb{F}_{2^9}$, costs $2{,}097$ \AND{} gates
for a field almost twice as wide, at the price of $1.5$M \XOR{}s per product.
Per-tree Merkle cap heights and a narrower AIR for the statement actually
verified reduce the hashing and column terms; the recursion of
Table~\ref{tab:recursion} does both, with a preprocessed tree read deep below its
root and tables whose opened values are linear forms of their columns.
At the other end of the graph, a Core-tested false-label-derived Taproot
pointlock reduces this implementation's 16-byte-label Disprove spend from
97 to 83\,vB when both outputs retain the same timeout sibling. This is only a
14-vB micro-optimization: it adds the Schnorr/discrete-log assumption, does not
reveal the false label and cannot by itself constrain the payout outputs.

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that the verification path exercised by one no-public-input
Keccak-$f$ configuration of Plonky3's Boolean-WHIR pipeline fits garbling well.
At $2^{18}$ rows it has 94.1M non-free gates and a 1.5\,GB garbled artifact,
about $30\times$ fewer non-free gates than the reported Boolean-garbled Groth16
verifier. This establishes the verifier-engineering result, not an end-to-end
Bitcoin protocol.

The largest measured on-chain component is revealing and authenticating the
1{,}041{,}024 circuit-input bits in a dispute, including their signatures or
preimages and verification scripts, rather than committing a Taproot output or
transporting the 127.1-KiB raw encoding. The executed components accounted here span
2.22--17.27\,MvB for one input-key set, where 2.22\,MvB is the 8-bit adaptor
baseline, with explicit but different output
shapes. Strict Core 31.1 accepted their staged funding--reveal slices, which
total 2.228, 11.883 and 17.311\,MvB. Lamport's additional tested join raises
its bounded slice to 17.324\,MvB, but the total protocol footprint remains unmeasured.
Hash-based multi-copy soldering is unimplemented, and the
independent seven-set component projection is 82.94--120.86\,MvB before the
transaction graph. The approximately 52-bit
quantum figure applies to the measured proof-system parameters under the stated
assumptions, while cut-and-choose is configured at 40 statistical bits and the
current transaction authorization is not post-quantum. Before deployment, the
construction needs public-input support, an implemented and measured multi-copy
input layer, a serialized and policy-tested Assert graph, post-quantum graph
authorization, and a measured parameter set with an enlarged field and labels,
a wider-state hash and stronger cut-and-choose.

The input shrinks further when the statement is itself a recursion proof.
Verifying the last of three narrow-recursion proofs of Ziren's binary stage
takes 564{,}552 input bits and 218.0M non-free gates, measured on real proofs
but resting on experimental, unreviewed changes to Plonky3 and Ziren: shared
ring-switch tensors, 200-bit digests, linear-form openings with affine
preprocessed forms and eq-factored bus rounds.
